Production strategies

The kinds are the structure of an explainer. These are the craft moves, and they are a second axis: any of them can go with any kind. Each one below was pulled out of a site on the kinds page or the index, and every one has a live example. Nothing here is hypothetical except the cells in the grid marked as untried.

The finding that jumps out: our eleven field maps contain no pictures at all. No illustrations, no diagrams, no charts; neither does the Man in the Arena. Every visual strategy we have proven lives in the one-page explainers, the parable, the atlas and the bake-offs.

Drawing S01–S05

S01

Metaphor cartoons that argue

Each abstract point gets one drawing that makes the point as a picture, so the drawing could replace the paragraph.

Why it works
A metaphor is an argument the reader can check at a glance: a developer fishing from a boat labelled “chat” above submerged assumptions says what the section says, faster, and it sticks. Decoration can be deleted without loss; these cannot. Nadeem even borrows a meme format (the midwit bell curve) when the argument is that shape.
What it costs
The expensive part is the brief: one sentence naming the metaphor, the labels and what the reader should conclude. Rendering is cheap. Gutter owns the house raccoon style (gen-image, Gemini 3 Pro, about $0.13 and 40 s an image); Muse Image is about $0.01 and 26 s; GPT Image 2.5 runs $0.10 to $0.26. magick cuts the background to transparent. Expect two or three renders per keeper.
Live examples
Plan mode is dead ↗ (seven cartoons, from the waterfall to the gravestone); Hierarchy of Money, fiat chapter (“claims for black money pile up; the stones do not”).

S02

A recurring cast

The same characters, drawn the same way, in every picture, with a cast page that introduces them once.

Why it works
The reader learns who stands for what once (the raccoon is the bank, the tortoise the central bank) and then every later panel reads instantly. A cast also turns drift into a checkable defect: one red claw, the right shirt.
What it costs
A reference sheet per character and a written canon, then every render is conditioned on it. Gutter and gen-image take the reference images; cheaper models drift (the Playground scores Muse and Recraft as “drifted” on style). Budget a re-render pass and a canon check per image.
Live examples
Hierarchy of Money, the cast; The Den (Larry, Igor and the attic, every week); Gutter Model Playground.
Pairs with
parablebake-offpost-mortemcase studyclose readingatlas
S03

Full-sentence alt text

Every image's alt text is a sentence, or a paragraph, that says what the picture argues, not what it depicts.

Why it works
Read the alt texts alone and you should have the argument; that is the test that the pictures are doing work. It serves screen readers, search, and the next model that reads the page. Writing it also exposes cartoons that do not actually argue anything.
What it costs
Minutes per image, no tools. The Den writes panel-by-panel alt with every speech balloon quoted. Our weak spot: the chroma-key page and the Hierarchy of Money cast portraits use short labels.
Live examples
Why task state needs Dolt (six diagrams, paragraph alts); Hierarchy of Money scenes; Gutter Model Playground; Nadeem's seven cartoons.
Pairs with
anything with a picture
S04

Arrow chains instead of paragraphs

A process written as one line of arrows, set against a second chain, so the shape of the contrast is the argument.

Why it works
chat → disambiguate → generate spec → review spec → revise spec → approve → implement → review code against understand → act → inspect → clarify → adjust → act again. One is a waterfall and one is a loop, and you see it before you read it. The prose around it only annotates.
What it costs
Nothing but discipline: finding the fewest steps that are still true. Monospace in the page; optionally redrawn as a cartoon, which Nadeem also does.
Live examples
Plan mode is dead ↗, the two loops. Our sites use arrows only inside prose.
Pairs with
post-mortemcase studyone questionfield mapatlasclose reading
S05

Mechanism diagrams

A hand-built diagram of the moving parts: which ref points where, what gets merged, where it breaks.

Why it works
Some explanations are topological. “Two agents commit the same SQLite file and git cannot merge it” is one picture and three confusing paragraphs.
What it costs
Inline SVG written by hand or by an agent, then checked in Playwright at phone width. Free to render, slow to get right; image models are bad at labelled diagrams, so this is not a job for Gutter.
Live examples
Why task state needs Dolt (rollback, dual sync, refs topology).

Shape S06–S09

S06

The ten-second skeleton

The whole argument is readable from the top of the page: a TL;DR, or a list of reasons whose items reappear as the section headings.

Why it works
The skimmer gets the conclusion and the reader who stays gets a map of where each claim is defended. Nadeem lists four reasons he was wrong, then gives each one a section with the same heading; skim the headings and you have the post.
What it costs
An editing pass, no tools. The risk is a TL;DR that summarises the topic instead of stating the claim.
Live examples
Plan mode is dead ↗; the TL;DR box on every page of Stevey, Mapped and The Man in the Arena.
Pairs with
every kindstrongest on post-mortem and field map
S07

Before and after, paired

Two things that differ in exactly one way, shown next to each other, so the difference is the lesson.

Why it works
A controlled comparison is the cheapest proof there is. Same person, same app, same silence gate: through AirPods only 0.35 s of a spoken “six” survives and the recogniser hears “Sex”; through a USB mic every word comes through whole. The reader draws the conclusion before you state it.
What it costs
Holding everything else fixed, which is the hard part. Then the capture: ffmpeg for audio, Playwright for screenshots, magick to lay two images side by side at the same size.
Live examples
When AirPods Eat Your Words (Exhibit A, Exhibit B); Chroma-key hill-climbing (on white against the alpha mask); Let's build GPT, part 6 (the numbers here against the finished script); Nadeem's two loops.
Pairs with
case studypost-mortemone questionfield mapatlas
S08

The honest objection

A section, near the end, that argues against the page: the one caveat, the critiques, the shadow side.

Why it works
It is what makes the rest trustworthy. A page that names its best counter-argument has shown it looked for one. Nadeem's post is the counter-example: it never makes the case for when a plan still pays, and that is its main gap.
What it costs
Research time, and a writer brief that demands it; left to themselves, writers skip it.
Live examples
“The one honest caveat” on every Yegge page; “The one honest objection” on Brooks; six critiques of the Arena; the shadow section of Why We Invented Clowns.
Pairs with
field mapclose readingone questionpost-mortematlasbake-off
S09

Best lines, cited

The source's own sharpest sentences, quoted verbatim and linked to where they appear, collected so they can be found again.

Why it works
Our prose explains, and the author's lines are what the reader remembers and repeats. A cite link on every quote keeps it honest and sends traffic back.
What it costs
Checking each quote against the original or an archive mirror. Auto-captions mangle names, so quotes from video need a listen.
Live examples
Stevey, Mapped: quotes (176 blockquotes, each cited); “The lines worth keeping”.
Pairs with
field mapclose readingparablebake-off

Evidence S10–S15

S10

Receipts under the joke

Every funny or pointed claim links to the real transcript it came from, one click down.

Why it works
The rule is Steve Yegge's and the Den keeps it: “If I can't produce the transcript, I don't get to draw the strip.” The joke lands harder because it happened, and the reader can check.
What it costs
A cleaned, public-safe transcript per claim, usually a gist, and a privacy pass before it ships. The receipts also have to exist before the drawing, which rules out anything made up.
Live examples
The Den (every strip carries a receipts block); Yegge's comic strip ships its receipts. Not yet used on any kind on the spine.
Pairs with
post-mortemcase studyparableclose reading
S11

Playable exhibits

The evidence itself, in the page, one tap to play: the audio clip, not a description of it.

Why it works
Hearing “six” arrive as a 0.35-second sliver settles an argument a waveform chart cannot. The reader stops taking your word for it.
What it costs
ffmpeg to cut clips, embedded as WAV (Playwright's Chromium cannot decode AAC, so test with WAV). TTS samples cost about $0.02 for five seconds of speech.
Live examples
When AirPods Eat Your Words (six clips); Larry Voice Catalog (twenty-odd voices, each with its own player).
Pairs with
case studybake-offfield mappost-mortem
S12

Timestamped source links

Every claim about a video links to the second it is said.

Why it works
It makes the page a better way into the source rather than a replacement for it, and every doubtful claim is one click from being checked. CS336 carries 1,618 of them.
What it costs
yt-dlp auto-captions (per video, then deduped), and one writer per chunk who verifies their own timestamps. Captions mangle proper nouns, so names get checked.
Live examples
CS336, lecture 1; Let's build GPT; Entropy, Mapped. Brooks links 166 episodes but without timestamps.
Pairs with
field mapclose reading of a speech on videoatlasbake-off
S13

The transcript as its own page

The cleaned full text ships alongside the explainer, anchored by paragraph, so explanation and source are both one link away.

Why it works
It lets the explainer stay short and original: the source is there for anyone who wants the words. For a close reading, the full text is the object of study.
What it costs
Cleaning the captions into paragraphs with timestamp anchors; Parakeet for audio with no captions. Cheap once the timestamp pipeline exists.
Live examples
CS336 transcripts; Entropy, Mapped; the full 1910 speech.
Pairs with
field mapclose readingcase study (the session log)one question
S14

Line-linked code and data

Code is annotated against line ranges in the real repo, and every data row gets its own permalink back to the line it came from.

Why it works
“Line 69 does this” is checkable; “the model does this” is not. For a dataset, a permalink per row makes the page citable at the level people actually argue about.
What it costs
A pinned commit so the line numbers do not drift, and a script to generate the anchors. The HarmBench table adds a search box and three filters over 510 rows.
Live examples
Let's build GPT, part 5; HarmBench, all 510 behaviors; CS336 assignment 4 (every deliverable linked to its test).
Pairs with
field mapone questioncase study (the diff)parable
S15

Checked against the original

The source is checked against itself (paper against repo, handout against code, the famous quote against the first printing) and every disagreement is shown, not smoothed over.

Why it works
It is where a derived page earns more trust than the source. Finding that the paper says 40/20 and the CSV says 41/19 tells the reader someone actually looked.
What it costs
A verification pass with a link sweep (“33 URLs checked, 32 live”). It is slow, and it is the part agents skip unless the brief makes it the acceptance test.
Live examples
HarmBench, where the sources disagree; the Arena against the 1910 printing; micrograd (notebook against video); Robert Moses Atlas (two of Caro's claims marked disputed).
Pairs with
field mapclose readingatlasparableone question

Comparison S16–S17

S16

The bake-off sheet

One frozen input through every candidate, outputs at the same size in a grid, with what each one really cost and how long it took.

Why it works
Only the tool varies, so the grid is the verdict. Measured cost per item ($0.010 to $0.255 an image on the Playground) beats a list-price table, and latency is often the deciding column.
What it costs
The renders themselves (the Playground cost about $0.96 for ten images), OpenRouter's billed cost field, and a stopwatch per call. Playwright to check the grid at phone width.
Live examples
Gutter Model Playground; Larry Voice Catalog; Chroma-key (each keying approach side by side); The Den, same script, three models.
Pairs with
bake-offone questionparableclose readingatlas
S17

A score you can climb

A written rubric or formula applied to every attempt, shown per attempt, so “better” means a number going down or a checkbox turning green.

Why it works
It turns taste into an eval. Chroma-key's score = residual × 5 + holes goes from 17,385 to 269; the Playground's five-point canon check (style, claw, beard, shirt, Crocs) makes model drift visible.
What it costs
Designing the metric, then scoring every output: a script for masks (magick), a zoomed-in look for canon checks. The metric can be gamed, so show the images next to the score.
Live examples
Chroma-key hill-climbing; Gutter Model Playground.
Pairs with
bake-offone questioncase studyclose readingparable

Interaction S18–S19

S18

A scrub control

One slider, playhead or step control that drives everything on screen, so the reader moves through time or through the steps themselves.

Why it works
Sequence becomes something you feel. Dragging from 1924 to 1968 and watching the expressways appear is the argument about accumulated power. On the parable, stepping through a chapter one move at a time shows the balance sheet change with each transaction.
What it costs
Real engineering: a dated, sourced event list, Leaflet or inline SVG, requestAnimationFrame, keyboard controls. Days, not hours, and Playwright tests for every breakpoint.
Live examples
Robert Moses Atlas (drag, play, arrow keys); Hierarchy of Money walkthrough (a 0 to 10 step slider).
Pairs with
atlasparablecase studypost-mortemclose reading
S19

A tested calculator

The answer as a live calculator with every assumption an editable field and every constant cited, and the arithmetic unit-tested.

Why it works
The reader's situation is never the example's. Letting them swap in their own numbers, with charts that redraw, turns a one-off answer into a tool, and the tests are why they can trust it.
What it costs
Plain JS and canvas charts, a Node test file per module (35 tests on cap-gains), and a citation for every constant. A day or two.
Live examples
Sell now or sell later (three sliders, eleven inputs, two charts).
Pairs with
one questionfield map (the napkin math)bake-off (cost at your volume)close reading
