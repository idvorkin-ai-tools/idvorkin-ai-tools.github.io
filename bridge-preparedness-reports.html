Preparedness reports, compared

Five labs now publish a document for each big model release that says how dangerous the model is. This page puts their frameworks and latest reports side by side: what they test, how they decide, who checks, what they hold back, and which evals they share. It starts from Meta's Muse Spark Safety & Preparedness Report. Back to the safety hub.

Each frontier lab pairs a standing framework (risk areas, capability thresholds, required safeguards) with a per-model report that applies it. This page compares the current pair from Meta, OpenAI, Anthropic, Google DeepMind and xAI: risk areas, threshold style, verdicts before and after safeguards, outside evaluators, redactions, cadence, and a matrix of the named evals each report cites. Before a company lets out a new, stronger AI, it checks whether the AI could help someone do something very dangerous, then writes a report card saying what it found and what locks it added. This page lays five companies' report cards side by side, so you can see how differently they grade. Back to the safety hub.

Every claim checked against the lab's own documents on 2026-11-21. These documents change often; dates are given so you can tell when this page has gone stale.

Not a constitution

A constitution or model spec, like Claude's constitution or OpenAI's Model Spec, describes the behaviour a lab is training toward: how the model should weigh helpfulness against harm, whose instructions win, when to refuse. It is a target, written once and revised now and then.

A preparedness report is a measurement. The lab tests a specific model for capabilities that could contribute to mass harm (weapons, cyberattacks, models escaping control), compares the results with thresholds set in a separate framework document, and records a decision: what safeguards the model needs, and whether it ships. A constitution says what the model should do; a preparedness report says what it can do and what the lab did about it.

So each lab has two documents: a standing framework that defines the risk areas, thresholds and required safeguards, and a per-model report (system card, model card or evaluation report) that applies it.

What stands out

The verdicts are not on a shared scale. For cyber, OpenAI calls GPT-6.2 Critical, DeepMind says Argon's CCL "cannot be ruled out", and Meta rates unmitigated Muse Spark 1.3 "moderate or lower". Part of that is a real capability gap (Meta's own table has Muse Spark 1.3 at 28.7 on ExploitBench capability coverage, against 73.5 for GPT-5.6 Sol), but each lab also defines its levels and chooses its tests differently, so the labels do not line up.

OpenAI's v2 framework says that for a Critical cyber model, "until we have specified safeguards and security controls standards that would meet a Critical standard, halt further development". The GPT-6.2 card declares Critical, ships with its strongest safeguards, and still cites v2; we did not find where it addresses that line.

Thresholds are moving in both directions. Meta's v2 changed critical risk from "Stop" to "Develop with Mitigations" but added numeric acceptance criteria. xAI deleted its numbers. Anthropic's v3 split its own commitments from industry-wide recommendations it "cannot commit to following ... unilaterally".

Persuasion is split: OpenAI dropped it, DeepMind and xAI track manipulation, Meta does not list it. The labs that measure evaluation awareness all report it: Apollo told Meta that Muse Spark had the highest rate it had seen, Anthropic saw it in up to 43% of audit transcripts, and DeepMind calls it pervasive in Argon's cyber and stealth tests.

How to read one

  • Find the two verdicts. One is what the model can do with no safeguards; the other is the risk left once safeguards are on. A headline "low risk" is usually the second one.
  • Check which model was tested. Capability tests often use a helpful-only or "unrestricted" variant with refusals stripped. A score on the shipped product mostly measures refusals.
  • Look for the threshold next to the number. A score means little until you know the cutoff it is judged against. Some labs print one; others say "holistic judgment".
  • Read the redaction and evaluator lists. What is withheld, and whether an outside group could disagree in print, tells you how much rests on trust.
  • Check the date and the framework version. Frameworks are revised every few months, and one model can be judged under rules that changed since the last report.
