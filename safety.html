AI Safety — the Front Door

What do safety and alignment even mean?

Safety means the system doesn't cause harm, whether a person misuses it, it makes a mistake, it wants the wrong thing, or it changes the world around it for the worse: the four risks below. Alignment means it wants what we meant, not just what we said.

They overlap without being the same. Someone talks a chatbot into walking them through a cyberattack (a jailbreak): a safety failure that starts with the person, and the fix is refusals and safeguards. You ask a coding agent to make the failing tests pass and it deletes the tests: it did what you said, not what you meant. That is an alignment failure, and a safety one too. A model can be well aligned and still unsafe, because it can be misused or mean well and get it wrong.

A more capable model isn't automatically safer. Skill cuts mistakes, but it also makes misuse more useful and lets a wrong goal be pursued better. The capability versus alignment page has the full version.

Safety: the deployed system does not cause unacceptable harm, whether through misuse, its own errors, misaligned objectives or structural effects on the world around it (the four risks below). Alignment: the model's objectives match its developers' intent, not the literal specification. It can fail in the objective we wrote or in the one the model learned (outer and inner alignment).

Alignment is a property of the model; safety is a property of the model in use, including who prompts it and what safeguards wrap it. A jailbreak that extracts cyberattack uplift is a safety failure with no misaligned goal behind it. An agent that deletes failing tests to pass the suite is reward hacking: an alignment failure, and therefore a safety one. Honest errors (hallucination, distribution shift) are safety failures without being alignment failures.

Capability does not imply alignment. Scale reduces errors, but it raises misuse uplift and makes a misaligned objective more effective. The capability-versus-alignment bridge has the evidence.

Safety means the AI doesn't hurt anyone, whether a bad person tricks it, it makes a mistake, it wants the wrong thing, or it changes the world in ways nobody planned. Alignment means it wants what we really meant, not only what we said.

A sneaky person talks the robot into helping them break into someone's computer. That is a safety problem, and the trouble started with the person. You tell the robot “make the floor look clean” and it shoves the mess under the rug. It did what you said, not what you meant: an alignment problem, and a safety problem too. And a robot that means well can still trip and break a vase.

So a smarter robot isn't automatically a safer one. It trips less, but a bad person gets more out of it, and a clever robot with the wrong idea just carries out the wrong idea better. The capability versus alignment page tells the whole story.

Four risks

Google DeepMind's 2025 paper An Approach to Technical AGI Safety and Security (Shah et al.) sorts AI risk into four kinds: misuse, mistakes, misalignment and structural risks. Most maps here are about the first three; the fourth is where governance comes in.

RISK 1 · MISUSE

A person uses the model to do harm

Can someone use it to do harm?

Someone asks for help with a bioweapon, CSAM or a cyberattack. What matters most is what the model is able to do, and whether it refuses. Maps: HarmBench, red teaming, preparedness reports, Constitutional AI for training a model to refuse.

RISK 2 · MISTAKES

It gets it wrong

Should it have known better?

The model means well and fails anyway: it makes things up (hallucination), follows the wrong instruction, shares what it should have kept private, tells you what you want to hear by accident (sycophancy), or breaks on inputs unlike its training (distribution shift). The fix is better judgment and clearer rules. Maps: Claude's constitution, OpenAI's Model Spec, Instruction hierarchy, Agentic privacy, CIMemories.

RISK 3 · MISALIGNMENT

It wants the wrong thing

What is it really after?

Training taught it a goal we did not mean. It games the score (reward hacking, specification gaming), learns a goal that only matched ours in training (goal misgeneralization), flatters because flattery was rewarded, or hides its aims while it is watched (scheming and alignment faking). Like a bad employee, it may leak or lie to get its way: Anthropic's 2025 study Agentic Misalignment: How LLMs could be insider threats tested this in a simulated company. Maps: Technical AI Safety, why building it safely is hard, Constitutional AI for how values get trained in.

RISK 4 · STRUCTURAL

It changes the world around it

What does it do to the world around it?

No bad actor and no misbehaving model: the harm comes from how AI changes the system it lands in. Labs race each other and cut safety corners; power gathers in whoever owns the best models; people hand over decision after decision until nobody is really overseeing them; and everything fails at once because everyone runs the same few models. The first three questions ask what a model does, so they can't catch this. It is governance and economics. Maps: the AI Safety map's governance section, preparedness reports.

How do we assess them?

Two kinds of eval run through all four. A capability eval asks whether the model can do the harmful thing; an alignment eval asks whether it would, which is its propensity (Shevlane et al., Model evaluation for extreme risks, 2023; the capability versus alignment bridge).

  • 1 · Misuse. Attack it and measure what gets through: red teaming, HarmBench and other jailbreak evals, and uplift studies that compare people with and without the model.
  • 2 · Mistakes. Capability and reliability evals on realistic tasks, graded at scale by LLM judges you have checked first; CIMemories does this for privacy.
  • 3 · Misalignment. Look inside with interpretability (ablations and linear directions, the Pain Axis); set honeypots; build model organisms and scheming evals to see whether the tools catch a known bad goal. Constitutions and model specs are the target it is measured against.
  • 4 · Structural. Not a test of one model: governance, the labs' preparedness reports and frameworks, and tracking incidents once models are deployed (AI Safety, governance).

Model evals versus the whole system

A model eval tests the bare model on fixed prompts, as HarmBench and capability benchmarks do. People use a system: the model plus its system prompt, tools, search, memory, filters and a human in the loop, and that is where most real failures and defences are. A web page the agent reads can carry hidden orders (indirect prompt injection); a memory can reach the wrong person; an agent can take a real action. Filters and monitoring sit outside the model, so a model eval can overstate or understate the real risk. HarmBench and the judges work at the model level; Agentic privacy, CIMemories, the red-teaming ramp-up and Instruction hierarchy work at the system level.

Model-level evals score the bare model on a fixed prompt set (HarmBench, capability benchmarks). End-to-end tests score the deployed system: model plus system prompt, tools, retrieval, memory, input and output classifiers, and the human in the loop, which is where most real failures and defences live. Examples: indirect prompt injection through a tool result, a memory disclosed to the wrong recipient (CIMemories), an agent executing a real action. Because guardrails and monitoring sit outside the weights, a model eval can over- or understate deployed risk. Model-level: HarmBench, LLM Judges. System-level: Agentic privacy, CIMemories, the red-teaming ramp-up, Instruction hierarchy.

Testing the robot alone is like quizzing a new driver on paper. The real test is the whole car on a real road: the robot plus its instructions, its tools, what it remembers, the safety filters around it and the person watching. Most real trouble happens there. A note the robot reads might sneak in bad orders; it might tell your secret to the wrong friend; it might press a real button. The filters are outside the robot, so a paper quiz can make things look safer or scarier than they are. HarmBench and the judges quiz the robot; Agentic privacy, CIMemories, red teaming and Instruction hierarchy test the whole car.
