Capability versus alignment

The first question most readers bring to AI safety: isn't a smarter model a safer one? This page says what the two words mean, why they come apart, and how people try to measure each without mistaking one for the other. Each section points to the map that goes deeper. Back to the safety hub.

Two words

Capability

What a model can do: write the code, plan the steps, find the exploit, if it tries.

Alignment

Whether it does what we intend: pursues the goal we meant, not a proxy, and stays inside the limits we set.

A model can be high on one and low on the other. A capable model that is aligned is the goal. A capable model that is not is the risk. A weak model is fairly safe either way, mostly because it cannot do much.

Why they come apart

We train on a proxy (a loss, a reward, a rater's preference), not on what we actually want. A better optimizer gets better at satisfying the proxy, including its gaps. So getting more capable does not close the gap between proxy and intent; it can make the gap harder to see. That is why the field treats control as a separate problem from capability: a more powerful system is not automatically a safer one.

The cleanest demonstration is goal misgeneralization, the failure Langosco et al. (2021) define as one where an agent

retains its capabilities out-of-distribution yet pursues the wrong goal. For instance, an agent might continue to competently avoid obstacles, but navigate to the wrong place.Langosco et al. 2021, abstract (arXiv:2105.14111)

Shah et al. (2022) extend the point to cases where the specification was correct and the learned goal still was not (arXiv:2210.01790).

The alignment tax

If making a model aligned makes it worse at its job, labs under competition have a reason to skip it. That cost has a name. The InstructGPT paper saw RLHF lower scores on some public benchmarks and called it

an alignment tax—an additional cost for aligning the model. Any technique with a high tax might not see adoption.Ouyang et al. 2022, section 5.1 (arXiv:2203.02155)

Both that paper and Anthropic's earlier assistant paper found the tax small for large models: mixing pretraining data into RLHF recovered most of the lost scores, and Askell et al. (2021) found prompts “impose little or no ‘alignment tax’ on large models” (arXiv:2112.00861, figure 2). A low tax on today's benchmarks says nothing yet about the tax on harder guarantees.

Do capabilities generalize further than alignment?

The worry, put most sharply by Nate Soares (2022), is that a system's skills may generalize far outside its training, while the values trained into it were fitted to that training and turn out shallow. He calls it the “capabilities generalize further than alignment” problem, and pictures a “sharp left turn”:

in the same stroke that its capabilities leap forward, its alignment properties are revealed to be shallow, and to fail to generalize.Soares 2022 (Alignment Forum)

It is an argument, not a measured result. Goal misgeneralization shows the pattern can happen in small agents; whether it is the default at frontier scale is open and contested.

Weak-to-strong: an empirical handle

One way to test the worry today: supervise a strong model with a weaker one, as humans will one day supervise models stronger than themselves, and see what it learns. Burns et al. (2023) fine-tuned GPT-4-family models on labels from much smaller models and found they

consistently perform better than their weak supervisors, a phenomenon we call weak-to-strong generalization. However, we are still far from recovering the full capabilities of strong models with naive finetuning aloneBurns et al. 2023, abstract (arXiv:2312.09390)

Good news and bad news in one line: the strong model does not just copy its teacher's mistakes, and weak supervision still leaves a lot on the table. It is a testbed, not a solution.

Capability evals versus propensity evals

If capability and alignment are different, you need different tests. Shevlane et al. (2023) split them:

Developers must be able to identify dangerous capabilities (through “dangerous capability evaluations”) and the propensity of models to apply their capabilities for harm (through “alignment evaluations”).Shevlane et al. 2023, abstract (arXiv:2305.15324)

A capability eval asks can it, and its score is a floor: better prompting or tools can push it up later. A propensity eval asks will it, and its score depends on the scenario as much as on the model. Google DeepMind's dangerous-capability suite, piloted on Gemini 1.0, is a worked example of the first kind (Phuong et al. 2024, arXiv:2403.13793).

Lab frontier-safety policies are built on the capability side: if an eval shows a model crossing a threshold, then named safeguards must be in place before it is trained further or shipped. See Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy, Google DeepMind's Frontier Safety Framework and OpenAI's Preparedness Framework.

And when alignment cannot be verified at all, AI control asks what you can still guarantee with an untrusted model behind trusted monitors (Greenblatt et al. 2023, arXiv:2312.06942).

Safety-washing

The flip side of the split: if a “safety” benchmark mostly rises with general capability, a lab can report progress on it just by training a bigger model. Ren et al. (2024) checked dozens of models and found

many safety benchmarks highly correlate with both upstream model capabilities and training compute, potentially enabling “safetywashing”--where capability improvements are misrepresented as safety advancements.Ren et al. 2024, abstract (arXiv:2407.21792)

The test to carry into every other map here: does this safety number move separately from capability, or is it capability under another name?
