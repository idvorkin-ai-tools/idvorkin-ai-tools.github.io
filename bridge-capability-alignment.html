Capability versus alignment

Capability is what a model can do. Alignment is whether it does what we meant. A model can have plenty of the first and little of the second. Back to the safety hub.

One example

Langosco et al. (2021) trained an agent on CoinRun, a platform game where the coin always sat at the end of the level. At test time they put the coin in random places. The agent still ran each level skilfully to the end and often skipped the coin. It had learned “go right”, which earned the same reward as “get the coin” on every training level. In the paper's words, the agent

retains its capabilities out-of-distribution yet pursues the wrong goal.Langosco et al. 2021, abstract (arXiv:2105.14111)

The authors call this goal misgeneralization. Nothing in training told the two goals apart, so more skill would not have fixed it.

Does the gap grow with capability?

Nate Soares (2022) argues that it does. Skills, he says, generalize far beyond training, while the values we trained in stay fitted to it. He expects a “sharp left turn” where

in the same stroke that its capabilities leap forward, its alignment properties are revealed to be shallow, and to fail to generalize.Soares 2022 (Alignment Forum)

This is an argument. Nobody has measured it at frontier scale, and researchers disagree about it.

Burns et al. (2023) ran a related experiment. They fine-tuned strong GPT-4-family models on labels written by much weaker models, a stand-in for humans supervising models smarter than themselves. The strong models beat their weak supervisors but stayed well short of what they could do with good labels (arXiv:2312.09390).

The alignment tax

If aligning a model makes it worse at its job, a lab racing its competitors has a reason to skip the step. The InstructGPT authors saw RLHF lower scores on some public NLP benchmarks and named the cost:

an alignment tax—an additional cost for aligning the model. Any technique with a high tax might not see adoption.Ouyang et al. 2022 (arXiv:2203.02155)

They recovered most of the lost score by mixing pretraining data into the fine-tuning. That result covers GPT-3-era models on public benchmarks.

Two kinds of eval

A propensity eval asks whether the model would do it, left to itself; a capability eval asks whether it can. Shevlane et al. (2023) call the first kind “alignment evaluations” and the second “dangerous capability evaluations” (arXiv:2305.15324).

A capability score is a lower bound, because better prompting or tools can raise it later. A propensity score holds only for the scenarios tested.

Lab safety policies trigger on capability evals: once a model crosses a threshold, named safeguards must be in place before it ships. AI control covers the case where propensity cannot be checked. It assumes the model may be working against you and asks which safeguards still hold (Greenblatt et al. 2023, arXiv:2312.06942).

Safety-washing

If a safety benchmark rises whenever general capability rises, a lab can report safety progress by training a bigger model. Ren et al. (2024) compared safety benchmarks with capability scores across dozens of models and found that

many safety benchmarks highly correlate with both upstream model capabilities and training compute, potentially enabling “safetywashing”--where capability improvements are misrepresented as safety advancements.Ren et al. 2024, abstract (arXiv:2407.21792)

Before trusting a safety number, check whether it moves independently of capability.
