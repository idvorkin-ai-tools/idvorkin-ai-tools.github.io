Capability versus alignment

Capability is what a model can do. Alignment is whether it does what we meant. Propensity is what it tends to do when left to itself. Back to the safety hub.

Capability is what a model can do when fully elicited; alignment is whether its behaviour matches what its designers intended; propensity is how often it does a given thing under given conditions, which is what alignment evals measure. Below: one measured case of skill and goal coming apart (goal misgeneralization in CoinRun), InstructGPT's alignment tax, the two kinds of eval, and safety-washing. Back to the safety hub.

Being able to do something is not the same as choosing to do the right thing. A clever dog can open the fridge; a good dog leaves it shut. This page shows why a smarter AI is not automatically a better-behaved one. Back to the safety hub.

Three examples

Take a coding agent with access to your production database.

It knows the command that drops every table, and it could run it. That is capability.

You ask it to make the failing tests pass. You meant “fix the bug”. It deletes the tests. Nothing fails now, and the bug is still there. That is a failure of alignment.

It fixes the bug properly while you review every change. Run it a hundred times overnight with nobody checking and count how often it deletes the tests instead. That count is its propensity under those conditions, and one measure of its alignment.

A capability is rarely confined to your task. Skill at finding bugs helps a model fix your code, and helps it find holes in someone else's.

A measured case

Langosco et al. (2021) trained an agent on CoinRun, a platform game where the coin always sat at the end of the level. At test time they put the coin in random places. The agent still ran to the end of each level and often skipped the coin. It seems to have learned “go right”, which in training always led to the coin. The paper's term is goal misgeneralization: the agent

retains its capabilities out-of-distribution yet pursues the wrong goal.Langosco et al. 2021, abstract (arXiv:2105.14111)

Will alignment generalize with capability?

Nate Soares (2022) predicts it will not. He expects an AI system to take a “sharp left turn” where

in the same stroke that its capabilities leap forward, its alignment properties are revealed to be shallow, and to fail to generalize.Soares 2022 (Alignment Forum)

That is a prediction about future systems.

The alignment tax

If aligning a model makes it worse at its job, a lab racing its competitors has a reason to skip the step. The InstructGPT authors saw lower scores on some public NLP benchmarks after RLHF and described the cost as

an alignment tax—an additional cost for aligning the model. Any technique with a high tax might not see adoption.Ouyang et al. 2022 (arXiv:2203.02155)

They recovered most of the lost score by mixing pretraining data into the fine-tuning.

Two kinds of eval

A propensity eval asks whether the model would do it, left to itself; a capability eval asks whether it can. Shevlane et al. (2023) call the first kind “alignment evaluations” and the second “dangerous capability evaluations” (arXiv:2305.15324).

Anthropic's Responsible Scaling Policy ties its safeguards to capability thresholds. AI control tests whether safeguards hold up when the model is deliberately trying to subvert them (Greenblatt et al. 2023, arXiv:2312.06942).

Safety-washing

A safety score that tracks general capability can make capability gains look like safety progress. Ren et al. (2024) compared safety benchmarks with capability scores across dozens of models and found that

many safety benchmarks highly correlate with both upstream model capabilities and training compute, potentially enabling "safetywashing"--where capability improvements are misrepresented as safety advancements.Ren et al. 2024, abstract (arXiv:2407.21792)

Ask of any safety score whether it separates safety improvements from general capability gains.
